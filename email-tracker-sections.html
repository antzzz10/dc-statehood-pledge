Where do your candidates stand on statehood? Early voting starts today.

Early voting is open through June 13, with the primary on June 16. Before you vote, we want to make sure you've had a chance to hear directly from the candidates — in their own words — on DC statehood and home rule.

Over the past several months, we asked 54 candidates running for DC Mayor, Delegate to Congress, City Council, and Attorney General to answer seven questions about their commitment to statehood and their specific plans to defend home rule. 27 responded. All 27 support statehood. But their approaches differ in meaningful ways — on strategy, on urgency, and on what they've actually done.

Robert White, running for Delegate to Congress, put it this way:

"Statehood is not a one-time vote — it is a sustained campaign. My work over the past decades reflects that reality."

The Delegate race, and what each candidate is actually planning to do.

Eleanor Holmes Norton is retiring after 35 years as DC's voice in Congress. Her successor will inherit the job at a moment when Republicans have introduced more anti-DC legislation in this Congress than in any recent session. Five Democratic candidates and one from the Statehood Green Party responded to the tracker — and they bring notably different approaches.

Trent Holbrook draws on eight years as Norton's senior legislative counsel:

"I took the bill from losing two-to-one to passing in the U.S. House of Representatives — twice. The second time, every single House Democrat voted YES for D.C. statehood."

His pitch is institutional knowledge and existing relationships. Read his full response →

Robert White emphasizes national coalition strategy and the current political moment:

"Right now seems to be the most compelling moment for us to successfully advocate and make DC statehood a reality. More Americans than ever before are aware of the fragility of American democracy."

Read his full response →

Brooke Pinto has been making the congressional rounds since January:

"I've been on Capitol Hill nearly every week since Donald Trump's inauguration championing the case of DC's self-determination in Congress."

She frames the path to statehood through building a national pro-statehood majority. Read her full response →

Kinney Zalesne is focused on the post-Trump political window:

"Once he is gone, America must rededicate itself to the principles of democracy — and I will argue that DC Statehood should be the #1 gesture and symbol of that recommitment."

Read her full response →

Greg Jaczko is proposing an interim step: eliminating federal income tax requirements for DC residents until statehood is achieved, creating parity with other non-voting US territories. Read his full response →

Kymone Freeman (Statehood Green) has practiced federal tax resistance for three decades and argues DC should immediately join other non-voting delegates in seeking tax exemption status. Read his full response →

The Mayor's race and a Congress that keeps coming for DC.

Since January 2025, Congress has moved to seize control of MPD, block locally-approved budget funds, and introduce 71+ anti-DC bills. The next mayor will spend their first term managing that interference while also trying to build the national case for statehood. We asked each mayoral candidate what they've done and what they plan to do.

Janeese Lewis George has been building congressional relationships one-on-one:

"Many Congress members have told me I am the first DC elected official who's ever met with them. That's a real shame. I worked with unions to help broker a meeting with a Democratic member of Congress who wasn't voting with DC — and that Congress member now consistently votes to support DC."

Read her full response →

Kenyan McDuffie is proposing a standing institutional response: a Home Rule Defense Task Force combining the AG's office, the Mayor's administration, and the CFO to proactively monitor congressional actions and prepare fiscal contingency plans. Read his full response →

Rini Sampath points to a structural vulnerability most DC residents feel but rarely see named directly:

"During the 2020 protests, federal authorities deployed the DC National Guard in ways that local leaders could not control. Unlike governors in every state, the Mayor of DC does not command the National Guard. The President does."

She also proposes exploring a DC–Puerto Rico dual-admission approach to address Senate opposition. Read her full response →

Gary Goodweather argues for pursuing every available path simultaneously — full statehood as the goal, but also revisiting the DC House Voting Rights Act approach for an interim House seat while the longer fight continues. Read his full response →

AG Brian Schwalb describes his response to the federal attempt to take over MPD last August — filing a lawsuit within 24 hours, and winning a rescission of the order within 48. Read his full response →

Primary day is June 16 — four days away. Full tracker: candidates.representdc.org

Council candidates, the special election, and who's running to lead the DC Democratic Party.

At-Large Council and the Special Election

This June's ballot includes both the regular At-Large Council seat and a special election for the seat vacated earlier this year. Several candidates in both races have responded to the tracker.

Elissa Silverman, running in the special election, lays out a coordination-first approach:

"Get our Council, Chairman, Mayor, DC AG, and Delegate all on the same page when it comes to statehood."

She also names specific congressional partners — Rep. Jamie Raskin and Sen. Van Hollen — and describes existing relationships with both. Read her full response →

Jacque Patterson, also in the special election, is proposing something less common — taking the fight to other states directly:

"This is how Home Rule was initially enacted — by opposing Rep. John McMillan in his home state of South Carolina, resulting in his defeat. DC cannot be the only battleground in the fight for statehood."

Read his full response →

Lisa Raymond, running for the regular At-Large seat, on leveraging DC voters who campaign in other states:

"Many DC voters work on Democratic campaigns in other states — we need to ensure they know which candidates have pledged to support DC Statehood and loudly refuse to volunteer for or donate to those who have not."

Read her full response →

Ward-level responses are also on the tracker from Charles Allen (Ward 6), Zachary Parker (Ward 5), Rashida Brown (Ward 1), and others.

DC Democratic Party Committee Races

On the same ballot are races for the DC Democratic State Committee — the party leadership body that shapes how Democrats in DC organize, spend, and message on statehood. Two main slates are competing: the Free DC Slate, running on a platform of grassroots organizing and direct confrontation with Congress, and Democrats United to Free DC.

Charles E. Wilson (Democrats United, At-Large Committeeman — and current DC Democratic Party Chair) on his record:

"I successfully led the passage of a resolution in which Democratic Party leaders from all 50 states and the territories pledged their support for advancing DC Statehood."

Read his response →

Slobodan Milic (Free DC Slate, Ward 4 Committeeman) has been organizing Washington Spirit fans as a statehood action:

"More than 15,000 fans chant 'Free DC' in the 51st minute of every home match, symbolizing our demand for DC to become the 51st state."

Kelsye Adams (Free DC Slate, National Committeewoman — co-founder of Long Live GoGo and Free DC):

"I believe Home Rule and Statehood are fundamental to racial justice, democracy, and dignity for the nearly 700,000 people who call this city home. I have spent my career fighting this fight with my whole heart."

The officials DC elects on June 16 will be managing that pressure, and building (or not building) toward the political conditions required to win statehood, for the next four years.

Oye Owolewa, running At-Large, on the stakes:

"DC statehood is about survival, dignity, and self-determination. The ongoing denial of statehood creates real harm — from budget instability to federal overreach and family separation — and our response must match the urgency of those conditions."
